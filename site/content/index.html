Echelon Foundry

Software built out of states, not out of hope.

Ordo is a State-Directed Engineering methodology. A system built under it says out loud what states it can be in, which changes between them are legal, what an actor is allowed to do next, and what is still owed. What the model does not permit is not a bug waiting to be caught — it is something the system does not offer.

How it works See the evidence

The one-minute version

Most systems represent what is true with a pile of booleans, nullable fields and status strings, and then defend that pile with tests. The combinations that should never occur are representable, so somebody has to remember to check for them — at every place that touches the data, forever.

Ordo starts one step earlier. Name the states. Declare which transitions between them are legal. Derive what an actor may do next from the same rules the transitions enforce, so the two can never drift apart. Make the crossing points to the outside world explicit, because that is where this research keeps finding the defects. Then verify with several mechanisms whose blind spots differ, because — as the evidence below shows — no single one of them is enough.

Ordo and SDE are the same methodology. Ordo is the name it carries in public. The research record that produced it, and every artifact cited on this site, calls it State-Directed Engineering. Historical names are not rewritten, because those names are part of the experimental record. The full explanation is here.

What this site will not do

It will not tell you Ordo makes teams faster. Nothing here measures that. It will not give you an industry-wide percentage, because three experiments in one organisation cannot produce one. Where a number exists, you can open the artifact it came from and check it. Where a number does not exist, the page says so instead of estimating.

That restraint is not modesty. A methodology whose central claim is that systems should make their own uncertainty explicit has no business hiding its own.
