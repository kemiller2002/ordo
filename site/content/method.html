Method

How Ordo works

Ordo is an order of operations, not a framework. It says where a change starts, what has to be true before it moves outward, which mechanism should refuse each class of mistake, and when to stop.

The construction order

{{diagram:engineering}}

Status of this ordering: experimental. It was assembled from results that are individually supported, but it has never been trial-tested as a single ordered workflow on a real project. It is recorded that way in the research map (CL-013) rather than presented as validated practice.

Classify the change before starting it

Ordo's first question about a task is what kind of change it is, because that decides where implementation begins.

Semantic change

The meaning changes

A new state, a new transition, a changed rule. Begins at the semantic authority — the one representation that decides the fact — and propagates outward from there. Starting at the edge and working inward is how two sources of truth get created.

Boundary change

A crossing point changes

A wire format, a persistence shape, an external contract. Needs one authority, a derivation from it, or a mechanical check that the copies agree. This is the class where this research found most of its defects.

Mechanical propagation

Consequences of a decision already made

The edits a semantic decision forces elsewhere. Ideally the compiler names every one of them. Measured at 8 of 12 on an unhardened architecture, and at every targeted site after hardening.

Presentation change

Only the surface changes

No state, no transition, no contract. The cheapest class, and the one most often mistaken for one of the others when a rule has leaked into a view.

Start at the authority, not at the symptom

The strongest-supported rule in the whole methodology is also the least exciting: change the semantic authority first, then let the consequences propagate. It is classified REQUIRED and supported by three experiments (CL-001), with the caveat that it has never been tested outside one application and one language.

The reason it matters is measurable. When a fact has one authority, adding a case to it produces compiler errors at the sites that must change. When the same fact is independently maintained in three places, adding a case produces compiler errors at one of them and silence at the other two.

Let the machine refuse first

Ordo's verification order is cheapest-and-earliest first: whatever a machine can refuse without running the program, it should refuse before a person looks. On an architecture with exhaustiveness checking, building before searching is recommended practice — with a real limit attached.

The limit, measured: mechanical feedback substitutes for search only in the tiers the architecture actually guards. In the paired trial, 7 of 19 required sites fell in an HTTP tier with zero mechanical guard on either architecture, and that is the dominant reason the hardened condition's discovery rate came in below its own prediction. An unguarded tier is unguarded no matter how good the guarded ones are.

Then verify with mechanisms that fail differently

{{diagram:verification}}

Every row's right-hand column is what some later mechanism has to cover. Adding more tests of the kind you already have moves nothing into the left column.

The evidence for this is the clearest thing on the site. {{metric:M-GF2B-OVERLAP}} of a fresh reviewer's findings were already covered by a 90-test suite plus an adversarial, mutation-tested self-review, in a build whose author was being unusually careful. In the same programme, a build whose suite {{metric:M-GF2A-DEFECTS}} — the type checker found two, a manual smoke test found one, self-review found one, and the tests found none.

Record what ran, and what was never captured

An experiment that reports only its available metrics reports a filtered result. Ordo requires the opposite: a metric whose telemetry does not exist is labelled NOT OBSERVABLE and left visible.

Several numbers on this site carry that label. {{metric:M-GF2A-COST}} for one whole experiment, because the runtime exposed no token or cost counter to the running agent. {{metric:M-HN1-TELEMETRY}} for another. These are absences of data, not zeros, and the distinction is enforced in this site's own build: a metric that states no limitation fails the build.

Stopping

Ordo treats stopping as a decision with criteria rather than a feeling. Work stops when every acceptance criterion has a passing test that was written from the requirement text rather than from the implementation's output, when each verification mechanism in scope has run and its result is recorded, when what was not established is written down, and when the open questions the requirements left open are still open rather than quietly decided.

Both greenfield builds preserved all nine of their requirements package's open questions unresolved rather than guessing past them. That is the behaviour this rule exists to produce.
