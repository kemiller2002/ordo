Interpretation

On the metric this experiment tracked, the conventional build handled the hardest change better than the explicitly-typed codebase did: one build attempt, one test run, nothing that failed. The log records that headline and then spends the rest of the entry explaining why it is the wrong number to look at.

The change renamed a duration field and altered what its unit meant. The compiler found eight sites — every place the old member name appeared. It could not find, and did not find, the roughly fourteen places where duration arrived as a bare positional integer. There, the literal 10 had meant ten six-minute units and now meant ten minutes: a sixfold change in real duration, expressed in source text that did not alter by a single character.

Each of those had to be found by hand and then interpreted — was this 10 standing in for an hour, or was it already a small literal that means the same either way? Most were migrated correctly. Two were left untouched, and the log is careful about why: not because they were checked and found correct, but because they happened to remain valid in the new scheme by coincidence. That is the most instructive sentence in the trial.

Throughout, the build was clean and thirty tests passed. Nothing mechanical distinguished a correctly migrated call site from a silently wrong one. A developer trusting a green build as evidence of correctness — which is the ordinary and reasonable thing to do — would have had no signal at all.

The wider pattern across the whole rebuild says the same thing more generally. The compiler caught four of six rework-driving mistakes, and every one of the sharpest was a category it cannot reach in principle: two swapped string arguments of the same type, a stale revision captured in a loop, a signature change hidden behind an inferred local. All three were caught by a person deliberately re-reading a diff before running it. That discipline held perfectly here, by design — and the persona being simulated, a competent professional under deadline, is exactly the group least likely to apply it every time.
